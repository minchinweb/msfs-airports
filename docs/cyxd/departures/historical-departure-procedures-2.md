# Climb runway heading off Edmonton Municipal

Edmonton Municipal, later Edmonton City Centre (CYXD), has one departure whose altitudes, runway order, and “before proceeding on course” instruction can be quoted from a page: the Canada Flight Supplement for **20 November 2008 to 15 January 2009**. Departure priority is **runway 30, then 34, then 16, then 12**. Aircraft **climb on runway heading to 3,200 ft ASL** before proceeding on course; **turbojets climb to 4,500 ft ASL, except 4,000 ft ASL on runway 30** ([CFS, GPH 205, pages B230–B231](https://www.ivao.ca/charts/CFS/CFS_AD_E.pdf)). That cycle is eight years past a model set in 1940 to about 2000, and it is still the only procedure with numbers a pilot was told to fly. Inside the requested window the printed “procedure” was thinner. From the early 1940s through at least 1958 the track aid was the **XD four-course range on 266 kc**, and the departure was an airway clearance, with **Amber 2** the named route north and **Red 6** the short airway east of the range. By 1978 the aid had become an NDB north of the field, and the guide that was opened prints which runway to use, including a **12,500 lb take-off cap on runway 11** and calm-wind use of **16-34**, with no heading and no altitude ([*On Track*, 1978, via RCAF.Info](https://rcaf.info/rcaf-stations/alberta-rcaf-stations/rcaf-station-edmonton-municipal/)). The database title **EDMONTON City FOUR DEP (CYXD 4.)** has no recoverable legs. A scenery year has to be chosen, because the range stood west of the aerodrome and the later NDB stood north of it.

## The 2008 supplement prints the only climb altitudes

The block is on aerodrome pages **B230–B231** of the Canada Flight Supplement, effective **0901Z 20 November 2008 to 0901Z 15 January 2009**. The header is **CYXD EDMONTON CITY CENTRE (BLATCHFORD FIELD)**, reference **N53°34′21″ W113°31′14″**, variation **17°E**, elevation **2,200 ft**. The same volume’s cross-reference still answers the old name: Edmonton Muni is City Centre. An earlier search that reported the Alberta directory pages as unopened is superseded by this extraction of [CFS_AD_E.pdf](https://www.ivao.ca/charts/CFS/CFS_AD_E.pdf).

The preferential-runway paragraph, under procedures, is the departure. Except for air traffic control, the order of runway priority is departure **30, 34, 16, and 12**. Aircraft climb on runway heading to **3,200 ft ASL** ( **4,500 ft ASL** for turbojets, **except 4,000 ft ASL for runway 30** ) before proceeding on course. The printed abbreviation is **BPOC**. Arrival priority in the same sentence is **12, 16, 30, and 34**, and aircraft stay at or above **3,200 ft ASL** ( **4,500 ft ASL** for turbojets) until established on final. **Between 0000 and 0600 local, runways 12 and 30 are the preferred pair for both arrival and departure** unless traffic, wind, weather, or runway condition requires a less preferred runway. Right-hand circuits apply to **runways 30 and 34**. On a visual approach to runway 16, the aircraft stays at or above **3,400 ft ASL** until past the XD NDB. Field elevation **2,200 ft** makes the ordinary climb restriction **1,000 ft above the published elevation**. The block prints no climb gradient in feet per nautical mile, and no take-off visibility or RVR. The reference line includes the token **CAP**, so instrument procedures, including any take-off minima, were in the Canada Air Pilot. That chart was not recovered. A footer in the same extraction reads issue 159 and **July 5 2007** beside the end of the City Centre procedures; that is the best candidate for the page’s amendment date, and a two-column extract can pin a footer to the wrong page.

Some aircraft operations are restricted **between 2200 and 0700 local**, and run-ups are prohibited in that window except as part of the pre-take-off procedure. A December 2006 encyclopedia summary of the supplement cycle **23 November 2006 to 18 January 2007** calls 2200–0700 the strictest noise window and says the field stayed open twenty-four hours, with a Category I ILS on runway 34, a localizer back-course DME approach for runway 16, and runway 12/30 visual, PAPI on 12 and VASI on 30 ([Wikipedia, December 2006](https://wikipedia2006.classicistranieri.com/e/d/m/Edmonton_City_Centre_%28Blatchford_Field%29_Airport_e888.html)). The 2008 page itself restricts some operations in that window and leaves the airport open. The same 2006 summary lists departure frequencies north **119.500** and south **133.650**. The 2008 extraction prints terminal **119.5** and **226.3** and does not claim to have listed every frequency.

This 2008 text is the late form of a request already on the city’s books in 1984. A reading of the **9 October 1984** council minutes reports that the Municipal Airport Noise Policy Study asked Transport Canada to amend *Canada Air Pilot – West* so that **runway 30** would be the preferential noise-abatement departure and **runway 12** the preferential arrival, and asked that formal noise-abatement take-off procedures be added to the CAP ([Council Meeting No. 23](https://cityarchives.edmonton.ca/meeting-no-23-74)). The 2008 priority lists open with those two runways, and they add the altitudes. No supplement or CAP page between 1984 and 2008 was opened, so the year the altitudes entered the book is unknown. The later pass could not re-open the minute book; the summary is the earlier reading of that archive page.

Two national rules sit beside the supplement and add no track of their own. **CAR 602.105** requires the pilot to follow whatever noise procedures the Minister has specified in the Canada Air Pilot or the Canada Flight Supplement, including preferential runways, departure procedures, and restricted hours ([Justice Laws, SOR/96-433](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/sor-96-433/page-62.html)). **CAR 602.106** names this airport: every runway at **Edmonton City Centre (Blatchford Field)** is noise-restricted for take-off by a subsonic turbojet heavier than **34,000 kg (74,956 lb)** unless a noise-compliance certificate is on board, with emergency exceptions ([CAR 602.106](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-96-433/section-602.106.html)). That is a 1996-era certification gate. A Boeing 737 is over that weight, and 737s did depart this field, which fits aircraft that carried the certificate. The section assigns no heading. Edmonton International’s NADP wording and its “no right turn west of the Leduc NDB” restriction belong to that airport’s own plates ([Boeing, Edmonton International](https://www.boeing.com/content/dam/boeing/boeingdotcom/commercial/noise/edmonton_int.pdf)).

## The east range leg crossed the aerodrome on 060 degrees

At the start of the 1940 window the low-frequency range was already the airway aid. By February 1938 the radio-range staff worked the floor below the weather office in No. 1 hangar, and Department of Transport calibration flights in CF-CCT checked the nulls between the A and N sectors. Trans-Canada Air Lines’ inaugural flight left Edmonton on the evening of **1 April 1939**. On **21 October 1942** a new administration building opened with the range staff, the tower, and the forecast office in it ([Robertson, CMOS Bulletin, 2001](https://cmosarchives.ca/History/Edmonton_EarlyAviationServices2001.pdf)). The wartime departure, as the Edmonton airways centre described it on **16 August 1944**, was a clearance to use the airway, then position reports over successive ranges. The centre sat in the airport administration building, had been taken over from the Department of Transport a few months earlier, and commissioned in January 1944. The release’s example is a civilian clearance from Edmonton to a main field on the northern leg of the Northwest Staging Route. It prints no climb heading, no crossing altitude, and no runway ([City of Edmonton Historical Impact Assessment, 2009, quoting RCAF Release No. 12](https://webdocs.edmonton.ca/occtopusdocs/Public/Complete/Reports/Ex/CSAM/2009-06-24/2009DCM032-Att6.pdf)).

The courses themselves are in the 1941 US Civil Aeronautics Administration table of Canadian radio facilities. Edmonton is call **VFE**, **266 kc**, identification **XD**, range courses **173–A–240–N–340–A–95–N**, station to field **61° magnetic, 3.3 miles**, field elevation **2,185 ft** ([*Air Navigation Radio Aids*, 1941, p. P-52](https://books.google.ca/books?id=cuRGAQAAIAAJ&pg=RA9-SL16-PA52)). The 1945 Cut Bank flight-information sheet repeats XD on 266, the same **61° / 3.3 mi** station-to-field figure, and tower frequencies **278 kc** and **126.18 Mc**. Its broadcast cycle names Whitecourt, Edmonton, Penhold, and Vermilion ([*Flight Information Bulletin*, 1945](https://books.google.ca/books?id=sfBGAQAAIAAJ&pg=RA8-PA6)). The RCAF/Department of Transport aerodrome page of about 1942, read again from the British Commonwealth Air Training Plan compilation, gives variation **25°E**, elevation **2,185 ft**, hard runways **3-21 at 4,500 × 200 ft**, **11-29 at 5,700 × 200 ft**, and **15-33 at 5,700 × 200 ft**, and the line “Aerodrome **3.35 miles** from R.R. at **61° magnetic**,” with the range at **266 kc/s**, identification **XD**. That page does not print the four courses ([BCATP aerodrome maps](https://www.bombercommandmuseumarchives.ca/bcatpmaps.pdf); the same manual page is Vol. 2, p. 90, as cited by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton_City_Centre_Airport) and hosted as a scan at [RCAF.Info](https://rcaf.info/rcaf-stations/alberta-rcaf-stations/rcaf-station-edmonton-municipal/)). Construction from 1942 to 1944 lengthened and strengthened those runways and, by the end of October 1944, had the concrete virtually complete. The minutes mention a new runway and never give it a number ([Historical Impact Assessment](https://webdocs.edmonton.ca/occtopusdocs/Public/Complete/Reports/Ex/CSAM/2009-06-24/2009DCM032-Att6.pdf)).

The CAA lists each leg as the magnetic course flown toward the station. The reciprocal of the **240°** inbound course is **060°** outbound. The field bears **061°** magnetic from the station, so the east leg is the leg that passed over the aerodrome, within a degree of the published station-to-field bearing. With the page’s **25°E** variation, the four outbound courses work out as **east 060°M / 085°T**, **north 353°M / 018°T**, **south 160°M / 185°T**, and **west-northwest 275°M / 300°T**. Those true courses are arithmetic from the 1941 table and the 1942 variation. They are the tracks a clearance onto a named leg would have used. No altitude to cross the range was printed, and none is supplied here. No publication names a runway centreline as the aligned leg. A general wartime remark that one leg pointed at the nearest runway is not an Edmonton chart and names no Edmonton runway ([Roscoe, Northwest Staging Route radio](https://jproc.ca/radiostor/Teslin%20Airport%20Radio.pdf)).

The airway names that are printed attach to the centre’s control area, effective **1 October 1946**. Edmonton ATC controlled **Amber 2** from the US border to 25 miles west of Watson Lake, **Red 6** from the Edmonton range to 25 miles east of the Edmonton range, all of **Red 7**, and **Green 1** from 25 miles west of Cranbrook to 25 miles east of Medicine Hat, plus traffic within 25 miles of the Lethbridge, Calgary, and Edmonton ranges ([*Airman’s Guide*, 1946, p. 149](https://books.google.ca/books?id=fGMkiDRAWhUC&pg=RA4-PA149)). Red 6 is therefore the short airway east of this range, which is the side the east leg occupies, the leg that already crosses the field. Green 1 was controlled from Edmonton and ran across southern Alberta; it did not leave the Edmonton range. Amber 2 is the Staging Route airway. In a fall evening during **May 1955 to August 1958**, a USAF C-47 departed Namao on Amber 2 for Fairbanks and the regular Canadian Pacific DC-3 departed Edmonton on Amber 2 for Whitehorse; when the two conflicted, the clearance passed to Edmonton Radio was to **hold present altitude immediately** ([Watts, Edmonton Radio](https://radiocom.ca/z_station_Edmonton.htm)). That is the only separation instruction quoted. Assigning the west-northwest leg to Amber 2 north and the south leg to Amber 2 toward Penhold and Calgary fits the outbound courses, the towns on the 1945 broadcast cycle, and Watts’s “departed Edmonton on Amber 2.” No enroute chart in these notes prints those magnetic courses beside the airway numbers, and Red 7’s leg at Edmonton stays unassigned. A 1947 guide notes an Edmonton ground-controlled-approach radar in use during instrument conditions and does not say which aerodrome it served ([*Airman’s Guide*, 1947](https://books.google.ca/books?id=YCGGjPalI7cC&pg=RA16-PA49)).

The same Watts tour is the evidence that XD was still being worked as a range at the end of the 1950s. The range position was standard, with ILS control, and the desk monitored several local low-power holding beacons whose idents he does not give. International had not opened; that opening was **15 November 1960**, and he distinguishes Namao from Edmonton, so the watch was the Municipal terminal. Nationally, ranges were being turned into non-directional beacons on the same frequency and ident by the mid-1960s ([Roscoe](https://jproc.ca/radiostor/Teslin%20Airport%20Radio.pdf); [*Electronics & Communications*, May 1958](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/CANADA/Electronics-and-Communication/Electronics-&-Communications-1958-05.pdf)). No document dates that conversion at Edmonton. The 1941 station stood **3.3 miles from the field on a bearing of 61° from the station**, which places the transmitter **west** of the aerodrome. The 1978 and 2008 aids, below, stand **north** of the field. Identification **XD** and **266 kHz** survived the move. A Flying the Beams coordinate, **N53°39′15″ W113°32′17″**, taken from a former Department of Transport site on a 1961 topographic map, sits about five miles north and does not satisfy the 1941 bearing; its dates are unconfirmed ([Flying the Beams](https://flyingthebeams.com/map)). Locator **ZXD** and localizer **IXD** appear as named aids on the 2008 aerodrome page. Nothing in the 1950–1980 sources dates their commissioning, and Watts’s holding beacons are not identified as ZXD.

## Calm wind in 1978 selected runway 16-34

The in-window layout after the range era is the 1978 *On Track* sheet for Edmonton Muni, read from the guide’s own pages. Variation is **23°E**, elevation **2,200 ft**. Runways are **03/21 at 4,451 ft** concrete, with an area marked closed near the 03 end; **11/29 at 5,868 ft** concrete; and **16/34 at 5,700 ft** asphalt, all 200 ft wide. Every threshold is displaced. The landing distance read from that table is **3,814 ft on 03, 5,068 ft on 11, 5,190 ft on 16, 4,319 ft on 21, 4,681 ft on 29, and 5,160 ft on 34**, each paired with the reciprocal as the take-off runway. Right-hand circuits are **21, 29, and 34**, which puts the pattern northwest of 03/21, north of 11/29, and east of 16/34. VASIS served **11, 16, and 29**. The noise block, “unless otherwise directed,” says **runway 16-34 is used in calm wind**, **runway 11 take-off is limited to 12,500 lb gross**, and **runway 29 landing weight is 30,000 lb maximum between 04Z and 14Z** (daylight time 03Z–13Z). The page says to consult the Edmonton Special Procedures Chart. That chart was not found ([RCAF.Info, hosting the 1978 pages](https://rcaf.info/rcaf-stations/alberta-rcaf-stations/rcaf-station-edmonton-municipal/)). The same sheet places the airport **157° and 3.8 NM from the XD NDB**. At **23°E** that bearing is almost true north: the NDB is north of the field, close to the runway 16 extended centreline. The 2008 supplement’s **162° at 17°E** is the same true relationship, about **179° true**, so the NDB site was stable from 1978 to 2008 while variation dropped. It is a different site from the 1941 range.

The runway numbers move on the same variation. A drop from **25°E** about 1942 to **23°E** in 1978 raises the magnetic heading of an unmoved pavement by about two degrees, enough for a runway numbered 15 to be numbered 16, and not enough for 11 to become 12. The 1978 sheet still says **11/29**. The first opened page that prints **12 (121°) / 30 (301°)** is the 2008 supplement. The year of that renumbering was not found; it is after 1978. A 13 August 1973 council record, as read in the earlier notes, has the City and the Ministry of Transport agreeing which carriers would use Industrial and which would use International with aircraft over **12,500 lb**, the same weight as the runway 11 take-off cap ([Council Meeting No. 24](https://cityarchives.edmonton.ca/meeting-no-24-64)). The minute text was not re-opened later.

Jets left this runway system in numbers, and the written constraint on them was the runway and the weight, not a turn. Pacific Western’s 737 shuttle used the downtown field; in **1976** the airline had up to **fourteen 737-200 departures a day** nonstop to Calgary, and the runway lengths were described as demanding maximum performance from that aeroplane ([Wikipedia, airline history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton_City_Centre_Airport)). The 12,500 lb cap kept those aeroplanes off runway 11, the heading toward downtown. It left 16, 34, and 29 available. In 1982 the Environment Council of Alberta called Municipal the most disturbing noise source in the province, named the **Boeing 737** as the main contributor, and recommended the city consider ending regular large-jet use. The recommendation prints no heading and no altitude ([ECA82-16/RR](https://nonoise.org/epa%20index/roll12/roll12doc53.pdf)). From 1986 the city kept a microphone on CFB Griesbach, north of the field, to measure Municipal noise ([Bylaw 10240](https://pub-edmonton.escribemeetings.com/filestream.ashx?DocumentId=123684)). The microphone shows where the city listened. It does not publish a required track. No tower tape, and no pilot account, quotes an Edmonton clearance that says when to turn. The one tracked departure that was written up, a 2006 VFR Seneca, was at **8,500 ft ASL nine minutes** after leaving City Centre and then cruised near **260° magnetic** toward Prince George, with no initial heading in the report ([TSB A06W0111](https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/rapports-reports/aviation/2006/a06w0111/a06w0111.html)).

## City FOUR is a title without a path

PilotNav’s index for the closed airport still lists **EDMONTON City FOUR DEP (CYXD 4.)** next to the aerodrome chart and the late approaches: NDB runway 16 (GNSS), localizer back-course/DME runway 16, localizer/DME runway 34, and ILS or NDB runway 34 (GNSS). The converted page has no runway transition, heading, altitude, radial, climb gradient, or effective date ([PilotNav CYXD](https://www.pilotnav.com/airport/CYXD)). Searches for City ONE, City TWO, and City THREE returned no procedure at this airport, so earlier amendments of that name are not established. The numeral four is the usual amendment count for a procedure named City. That is a naming convention, and it is not a ground track.

Canada Air Pilot volume 3 is Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba, and the CAP series is where standard instrument departures are published ([NAV CANADA eCAP](https://products.navcanada.ca/shop-electronic-products/Electronic-Canada-Air-Pilot/)). Two public CAP 3 scans are identifiable by cycle: **13 April 2006 to 8 June 2006**, and **11 February 2010 to 8 April 2010**. Indexed text of the 2010 cycle shows Edmonton City Centre **NDB RWY 16 (GNSS)** at CAP page **94**, variation **17°E**, change note “Comm NAD83,” and does not show a City FOUR leg ([Yumpu CAP 3, 11 Feb 2010](https://www.yumpu.com/en/document/view/51763834/cap-3-canada-air-pilot-instrument-procedures-edmonton-fir)). Direct fetch of both scans returned 403, so unread pages of those uploads can still hold a departure the search index missed. An IVAO CAP 3 index lists CYXD as one chart bundle and does not print a plate URL. Missed-approach tracks on the approaches are approaches. Reversing NDB runway 16 or the ILS to runway 34 does not produce City FOUR.

The name is evidence that a SID was in the civil book at some point before closure on **30 November 2013**. It is a late plate. Nothing ties it to 1940–1980, and nothing in hand can be coded as its path. Runway 16/34 closed on **3 August 2010**; the 11 February 2010 cycle is the last opened CAP date on which the north-south instrument runway was still in the approach title.

## Conclusion

The scenery wants three procedures, stacked by year, and two different XDs. A Municipal of the 1940s and 1950s uses variation **25°E**, elevation **2,185 ft**, runways **3/21, 11/29, and 15/33**, and a range on **266 kc** standing **3.3 miles west** of the field. Eastbound traffic is already on the leg that crosses the aerodrome, outbound **060° magnetic**. A northbound clearance is “on Amber 2,” with an immediate altitude hold as the tool when Namao is on the same airway, and with no published crossing altitude to invent. A Municipal of 1978 uses variation **23°E**, the NDB **3.8 NM north** on the same frequency and ident, runways **03/21, 11/29, and 16/34**, calm-wind use of **16-34**, the **12,500 lb** cap on runway 11 take-off, and right-hand circuits on **21, 29, and 34**. A field set at the end of the century should take the 2008 headings, **12/30 at 121°/301°** and **16/34 at 163°/343°**, variation **17°E**, and the climb on runway heading to **3,200 / 4,500 / 4,000 ft ASL**, because that is the noise policy the city asked for in 1984 written out with altitudes, and it is the only departure a page actually specifies. The pattern north of the long east-west runway is the piece that holds still: right-hand on 29 in 1978, right-hand on 30 in 2008.

What changed is the aid and the kind of instruction. The range west of the field became the NDB north of it, sometime after August 1958 and before the 1978 guide, with the year itself unprinted. The instruction changed from “cleared on the airway” to “use this runway,” and only in the supplement that was opened does it become “climb runway heading to this altitude, then proceed on course.” City FOUR’s legs, any CAP take-off minima, and any supplement edition between the 1984 request and the 2008 page are the documents that would move those dates. Until one of them is in hand, mixing 12/30 headings with the 1941 range, or drawing a path under the name City FOUR, builds a procedure no source contains.
