# Radio-range era departures, Edmonton Municipal

Scope is Edmonton Municipal / Industrial / City Centre (later CYXD) only, 1940–about 1980. Namao is mentioned only where a source separates it from Municipal. Edmonton International (opened 15 November 1960) is not treated as this field.

The 1941 CAA quadrantal course table (173–A–240–N–340–A–95–N), the 61°/3.3 mi station-to-field figure, the c.1942 runway list, the 1946 coloured-airway control limits, and the 1978 *On Track* runway and XD-bearing figures are already written up in `primary-sources-1941-1978.md`. They are not re-derived here. This pass did not re-open those Google Books scans. A second image of the same c.1942 aerodrome page does confirm the runway list and the 61°/3.35 mi note already on file; it does not add range courses.

## What were the Edmonton radio range courses, the bearing and distance from the range to the field, and which runway aligned with a range leg, in 1941–1950s publications?

### Takeaway
No CAA, DOT, or RCAF table later than the 1941 course list already on file was found, and no publication in this pass names a runway number as the leg that overflew the aerodrome. What is new is where the range crew worked, that the range was still being calibrated and voice-broadcast in the 1930s–1950s, and a general wartime claim that one leg was aimed at the nearest runway.

### Cited Findings
- By February 1938 the Edmonton weather office was on the top floor of the tower in No. 1 hangar at the Municipal Airport, and the radio-range staff were on the floor immediately below. The teletype circuit then linked Edmonton (XD), Penhold, Calgary, and Lethbridge. Jack Hunter’s DOT calibration flights in CF-CCT checked “the bearing of the null points between the A and N sectors” of the Edmonton range. By March 1939 DOT had finished emergency fields, radio ranges, and weather stations on the Lethbridge–Edmonton feeder; TCA’s inaugural flight left Edmonton on the evening of 1 April 1939. On 21 October 1942 a new administration building opened and housed the radio-range staff, the control tower, and the forecasting office. — [Robertson, CMOS Bulletin SCMO 29(4), 2001, reminiscence of 1938–1950](https://cmosarchives.ca/History/Edmonton_EarlyAviationServices2001.pdf)
- A second scan of the c.1942 RCAF/DOT aerodrome page repeats figures already collected elsewhere: variation 25°E, elevation 2185 ft, “Aerodrome 3.35 miles from R.R. at 61° magnetic,” and hard runways 3-21 (4500×200), 11-29 (5700×200), 15-33 (5700×200). It does not print the four range courses. — [BCATP aerodrome-map compilation, bombercommandmuseumarchives.ca](https://www.bombercommandmuseumarchives.ca/bcatpmaps.pdf); same page cited by [Wikipedia, “Edmonton City Centre Airport,” as *Pilots Handbook of Aerodromes and Seaplane Bases* Vol. 2, p. 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton_City_Centre_Airport)
- Spurgeon Roscoe’s Northwest Staging Route radio history lists Edmonton as CYXD, range ident XD, 266 kc, “from the time they went in service,” in a table with Grande Prairie QU 221, Fort St. John XJ 326, Fort Nelson YE 382, Smith River ZL 263, Watson Lake QH 248, Teslin ZW 269, and Whitehorse XY 302. He gives the wartime RCAF communications call for Edmonton as VFDX, and says that when the RCAF turned the eleven stations over to DOT in 1951 the communications call became VFE. He does not print magnetic courses. The 1941 CAA call VFE is already in the earlier notes; Roscoe’s 1951 date is for the communications call after the RCAF handover, not a newly measured course. — [Roscoe, *Teslin Airport Radio / Northwest Staging Route Radio*, jproc.ca PDF](https://jproc.ca/radiostor/Teslin%20Airport%20Radio.pdf)
- Roscoe, writing about ranges in general and not from an Edmonton chart: “Apparently during the war one leg of the radio range pointed to the runway of the nearest airport.” He immediately says he has no knowledge of that applying at Teslin, and he never names an Edmonton runway. — [Roscoe, jproc.ca PDF](https://jproc.ca/radiostor/Teslin%20Airport%20Radio.pdf)
- Milton Watts, DOT radio operator at Edmonton Radio from May 1955 to August 1958 (account dated 30 June 2012), still worked a “range position” and made the weather broadcast from it. International had not opened (15 November 1960), and he distinguishes Namao as a separate field, so this range watch was the Municipal terminal. He gives no course numbers, no station-to-field bearing, and no runway alignment. — [Watts, radiocom.ca, “Edmonton”](https://radiocom.ca/z_station_Edmonton.htm)
- A May 1958 trade article says DOT expected VOR “from Kenora to Lethbridge and from Lethbridge to Calgary and Edmonton” by the end of 1958, and that “the older navigational aid, the low frequency radio range, is still in use across Canada.” It does not say the Edmonton range had been moved, re-coursed, or switched off, and it does not say whether the new VOR was at Municipal or on the International site then under construction. — [*Electronics & Communications*, May 1958, “DOT Installing VOR”](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/CANADA/Electronics-and-Communication/Electronics-&-Communications-1958-05.pdf)

### Inferences
- Labelled reconstruction, not a new measurement: the only runway-alignment statement found is Roscoe’s general wartime practice. Combined with the already-documented 61° magnetic / 3.3–3.35 mi station-to-field figure, that practice is consistent with one leg aimed at the aerodrome, but this pass still has no source that says “runway 11” or any other number was the aligned runway. The earlier note’s leg-by-leg table is that note’s reconstruction; it is not repeated here.
- Watts’s 1955–58 range watch is evidence the four-course range had not yet been reduced to a plain NDB by the end of his tour. It is not evidence the 1941 courses were unchanged.

### Gaps
- No second primary course table (1946, 1948, 1950, 1955, or a Radio Facility Chart rose) was retrieved. Whether 173/240/340/95 were ever swung is unknown.
- No surveyed latitude and longitude of the 1940s range site was found. The Flying the Beams coordinate already discussed in the earlier notes was not re-verified from that site’s data file.
- No publication found that names which physical runway centreline, as opposed to “the aerodrome,” lay on a range leg.

## Which airways left Edmonton, and how did a pilot depart the field and join one, Amber 2 northbound in particular?

### Takeaway
The documented “departure” was an airway clearance, then position reports over successive radio ranges. For Amber 2 northbound, a 1955–58 operator account says both a Municipal departure and a Namao departure were simply “on Amber 2”; when they conflicted, ATC’s tool was an immediate altitude hold, not a published climb track.

### Cited Findings
- RCAF North West Air Command Release No. 12, 16 August 1944, describes the Edmonton airways traffic control centre in the airport administration building. The centre had been taken over from DOT a few months earlier. “Its methods are based on standard airways practice which is virtually the same throughout Canada and the United States, so that any professional transport pilot accustomed to flying regular airways can bring his aircraft without difficulty into Edmonton.” “Every aircraft, flying for any purpose whatsoever, gets a ‘clearance’ to use the airway before it enters the vast area controlled by the Edmonton center. From that moment on, a report on its movements reaches the center from the radio range stations over which it travels. Estimated times of reaching the next reporting point are included in every report.” The same release’s example is a civilian pilot who “had a clearance from Edmonton to fly to one of the main airfields on the northern leg of the North West Staging Route.” Low cloud made “approaches for landings” the instrument problem; the release does not print a climb-out heading, a crossing altitude, or a runway. — quoted in the City of Edmonton *Historical Impact Assessment* (2009) from [LAC RG 24 Vol. 3241 File 202-4-8](https://webdocs.edmonton.ca/occtopusdocs/Public/Complete/Reports/Ex/CSAM/2009-06-24/2009DCM032-Att6.pdf)
- The same assessment, from LAC files, places the Edmonton centre’s commissioning in January 1944, after the RCAF took over administration of the Edmonton airport and the Northwest Staging Route from DOT in September 1942. On 17 October 1945 Cabinet left DOT, along that route, responsible for “meteorological and radio range services.” A January 1946 speech quoted there says the route “was beamed throughout.” None of these give leg degrees at Edmonton. — [Historical Impact Assessment, 2009](https://webdocs.edmonton.ca/occtopusdocs/Public/Complete/Reports/Ex/CSAM/2009-06-24/2009DCM032-Att6.pdf)
- Watts, fall evening during his May 1955–August 1958 tour at Edmonton Radio: a USAF C-47 “departing Namao Air Base headed on Amber 2 airway to Fairbanks,” then “the regular CPA DC3 flight departed Edmonton on Amber 2 headed for Whitehorse.” The C-47 later “turned and was headed back on Amber 2 descending to Namao.” ATC passed Edmonton Radio a clearance for the CPA flight to “hold their present altitude immediately.” The CPA crew then reported, “Who was that, they just passed overhead.” Watts distinguishes Namao Air Base from “Edmonton.” He does not give the outbound course, the range leg, or a climb altitude. — [Watts, radiocom.ca](https://radiocom.ca/z_station_Edmonton.htm)
- Roscoe, describing the Yukon end rather than Edmonton: Amber Two was “the air route that went up the Alaska Highway and into Alaska.” The Watson Lake west on-course signal pointed at Whitehorse, and the Whitehorse east on-course signal pointed at Watson Lake. Teslin’s east and west signals formed Red Five (Teslin–Watson Lake), not an Edmonton airway. Ferry fighters on the Staging Route “were not allowed to use the radio range”; “the delivery pilots were flown back by a C47 … and they were the aircraft using the radio ranges.” — [Roscoe, jproc.ca PDF](https://jproc.ca/radiostor/Teslin%20Airport%20Radio.pdf)
- Robertson (2001), separating the fields: Namao was authorized in 1943, completed in 1945, and the USAAF moved its flying and ferrying, including its weather office, there from Municipal. He places it “some 15 km north of Edmonton.” Staging Route ranges he names are Grande Prairie, Fort St. John, Fort Nelson, Watson Lake, and Whitehorse, with Snag, Aishihik, Teslin, Smith River, and Beatton River added by 1943. The Mackenzie route fields he names are Fort McMurray, Fort Smith, Fort Simpson, Fort Norman, and Norman Wells. He does not assign those routes to Green, Amber, or Red numbers at the Edmonton range. — [Robertson, 2001](https://cmosarchives.ca/History/Edmonton_EarlyAviationServices2001.pdf)
- Outside the 1940–1980 window, but the only sequenced list found of the later LF airway through the Edmonton NDB: a 1 August 2008 quotation of the *Designated Airspace Handbook* gives LF/MF airway A2 as Calgary NDB to Red Deer NDB to Edmonton NDB to Whitecourt NDB to Grande Prairie NDB to Dawson Creek NDB to Fort St. John NDB to Fort Nelson NDB to Liard River NDB to Watson Lake NDB to Whitehorse NDB, and eventually Beaver Creek NDB to Nabesna. No magnetic courses are quoted. — [Blake Crosby, 1 August 2008](https://fly.blakecrosby.com/2008/08/overlapping-airways.html)
- Also outside the window: NAV CANADA’s 25 April 2019 revocation list still has A2 segments Whitecourt NDB–Grande Prairie NDB–Dawson Creek NDB–Fort St. John NDB–Fort Nelson NDB, and a different route, A7, from XD (Edmonton) NDB to DETBA to RAGUR to Peace River NDB. The 2019 list does not include an Edmonton–Whitecourt segment. These are not 1940–1980 procedures. — [NAV CANADA, Notice of Change, LF air routes, effective 25 April 2019](https://www.navcanada.ca/en/notice-of-change-2019-lf-air-route-revoke-en.pdf)

### Inferences
- Labelled reconstruction of the join, from the 1944 release plus the Watts incident, not from a plate: the pilot received a clearance to a named airway (Amber 2 for the northbound Staging Route), left the field, and was identified thereafter by estimates and reports over the ranges along that airway. The only separation instruction actually quoted is “hold their present altitude immediately.” No source gives the outbound magnetic course the Municipal departure flew to intercept Amber 2, or a “cross the range at” altitude.
- The 1946 *Airman’s Guide* control limits (Amber 2, Red 6, Red 7, Green 1) stay in the earlier note. This pass did not find a chart that assigns Red 6 or Red 7 to a named Edmonton range leg, so that earlier leg-to-airway table is still unconfirmed.
- The 2008 A2 string (Edmonton NDB–Whitecourt–Grande Prairie and south via Red Deer–Calgary) is the later LF name of the same corridor Watts called Amber 2. It is not a 1950s course.

### Gaps
- No enroute chart, Radio Facility Chart, or *Canada Air Pilot* extract was found that prints the magnetic course of Amber 2, Red 6, or Red 7 off the Edmonton range, or a Victor airway radial off Municipal.
- No clearance strip or phraseology example was found that says “climb runway heading,” “after takeoff turn left to intercept,” or “cross XD at or above” a stated altitude.
- Green and Blue airways through the Edmonton range were not found. The 1946 Green 1 limit in the earlier note does not pass the Edmonton range.

## When did the range become the XD NDB, when did IXD and ZXD appear, and did any 1950–1980 document print a departure, hold, or climb-out?

### Takeaway
The Edmonton range was still being worked as a range in 1955–1958, and that same position already had ILS control and monitored unnamed low-power holding beacons. No 1950–1980 DOT, RCAF, or *Canada Air Pilot* page found here prints a Municipal departure route, a holding pattern, or a standard climb-out, and none of the sources name IXD or ZXD.

### Cited Findings
- Watts, May 1955–August 1958: “The range position was standard, with ILS control. We monitored several local low power holding NDB.” One of those beacons was on the same frequency as the Cambridge Bay NDB and was sometimes covered by it. He does not give the local ident, the frequency, or a holding radial or timing. A third air-ground position was added later in his tour for CPA, Pacific Western, and Eldorado. He also records operators being tested as radar technicians during the same years (“Sometime during my stay in Edmonton we became acquainted with Radar”), without saying a departure was radar-vectored. — [Watts, radiocom.ca](https://radiocom.ca/z_station_Edmonton.htm)
- Roscoe’s general chronology, not an Edmonton commissioning notice: ADF “began to replace the radio range receiver” in the 1950s; “by the mid 1960s” ranges “were being converted to a non-directional beacon” on the same frequency and ident. At Teslin the range in his 1963 photograph was replaced “a few years” later and the NDB was moved onto the airport. He does not date Edmonton’s conversion. He still calls Edmonton’s 266 kc facility a radio range in the ident table. — [Roscoe, jproc.ca PDF](https://jproc.ca/radiostor/Teslin%20Airport%20Radio.pdf)
- May 1958: low-frequency ranges still in use in Canada while VOR was being extended toward Edmonton by the end of that year. — [*Electronics & Communications*, May 1958](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/CANADA/Electronics-and-Communication/Electronics-&-Communications-1958-05.pdf)
- The August 1944 RCAF release, above, describes instrument approaches and airway clearances. It does not describe a standard instrument departure, a published hold, or a climb gradient. — [Historical Impact Assessment, quoting Release No. 12](https://webdocs.edmonton.ca/occtopusdocs/Public/Complete/Reports/Ex/CSAM/2009-06-24/2009DCM032-Att6.pdf)
- Modern navaid directories still show XD as an NDB on 266 kHz at 53°38′37″N 113°30′47″W, ZXD (Blatchford) on 201 kHz at 53.499401, −113.513000, and IXD as the Edmonton Muni localizer/DME on 109.50 (DME channel 032X). They are present-day or recent-database positions. They are not commissioning dates, and they are not evidence of where the 1940s range stood. — [PilotNav XD](https://www.pilotnav.com/navaid/sps-10552); [OurAirports ZXD](https://ourairports.com/navaids/ZXD/Blatchford_NDB_CA/); [PilotNav IXD DME](http://www.pilotnav.com/navaid/sps-4375)

### Inferences
- Labelled inference: because Watts still broadcast weather from a range position through 1958, the Edmonton low-frequency range had not yet become “only an NDB” by August 1958. The conversion, if it followed Roscoe’s national pattern, was a mid-1960s event. That date is not documented for Edmonton.
- Labelled inference: “ILS control” on the Municipal range position in 1955–58 means the terminal radio desk could control an ILS before International opened. It does not identify the runway, the ident IXD, or the year the transmitter was flight-checked. The holding beacons he monitored are not identified as ZXD.
- No document in this pass is a departure procedure. The nearest operational texts remain the 1944 airway clearance and the 1955–58 “departed Edmonton on Amber 2” / “hold their present altitude” exchange.

### Gaps
- Commissioning or cancellation dates for the Edmonton four-course range, the XD NDB, locator ZXD, and ILS IXD were not found in a DOT, RCAF, or *Canada Air Pilot* issue from 1950–1980.
- No holding-pattern entry, inbound course, or timing for Edmonton was found. Watts confirms that local low-power holding NDBs existed; he does not describe how to fly them.
- *Canada Air Pilot* and Radio Facility Chart pages for Edmonton Municipal were not located online in this pass. HathiTrust and Library and Archives Canada copies were not opened.
- The 1978 “157° 3.8 NM from XD NDB” figure stays in the earlier note. This pass did not find an earlier NDB-era bearing to compare with the 1941 61°/3.3 mi range-era bearing.

## What runway layout applied in each decade?

### Takeaway
Only two dated layouts are in primary aerodrome publications already identified: 3/21, 11/29, and 15/33 about 1942, and 03/21, 11/29, and 16/34 in 1978. Wartime files describe strengthening and a new runway in 1942–44 without new numbers. The 12/30 designation is later than 1978; this pass did not find the chart that first printed it.

### Cited Findings
- About 1942: runways 3/21, 11/29, and 15/33, lengths 4500, 5700, and 5700 ft, all 200 ft wide, hard surfaced; variation 25°E; elevation 2185 ft. Already in the earlier note; confirmed again on the BCATP scan and on the Wikipedia citation of the same manual page. — [bcatpmaps.pdf](https://www.bombercommandmuseumarchives.ca/bcatpmaps.pdf); [Wikipedia, citing Vol. 2, p. 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton_City_Centre_Airport)
- 1942–44 construction, numbers not restated: on 25 June 1942 the Edmonton airport was reported to have bituminous runways, with further US-requested work to include “construction of a new runway, widening existing runways and extending taxi-strips.” In September 1942 blacktop “did not stand up to the weight of the four engined B.24,” and steel mat was laid; DOT was lengthening and strengthening the runways. In May 1944 the bituminous runways began conversion toward concrete; by the end of October 1944 “the concrete runways were virtually complete.” — [Historical Impact Assessment, 2009, citing LAC RG 12 and RG 24](https://webdocs.edmonton.ca/occtopusdocs/Public/Complete/Reports/Ex/CSAM/2009-06-24/2009DCM032-Att6.pdf)
- 1978 *On Track* still shows 03/21, 11/29, and 16/34. That page is already transcribed in the earlier note and was not re-measured here. It is the latest in-window layout found, and it still uses 11/29 rather than 12/30.
- The current Wikipedia aerodrome table, sourced to a *Canada Flight Supplement* cycle dated 27 November 2025–22 January 2026, lists 12/30 at 5,870 ft and 16/34 at 5,700 ft, elevation 2,202 ft. That is the late field, not a 1940–1980 chart, and the CFS date is after the airport closed. — [Wikipedia, “Edmonton City Centre Airport”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton_City_Centre_Airport)
- Wikipedia, citing an Alberta aviation-heritage page, says that on 1 October 1955 RCAF squadrons and support units moved from Blatchford to RCAF Station Namao. That is a change of who flew from the field, not a runway-number list. — [Wikipedia, “RCAF Station Edmonton”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton_City_Centre_Airport)

### Inferences
- Labelled reconstruction from the two published variation figures already on file (25°E about 1942, 23°E in 1978): a two-degree decrease in easterly variation raises the magnetic heading of an unmoved runway by about two degrees. A runway numbered 15 while its magnetic heading sat near 153–154° can become 16 without being rebuilt. The same two degrees cannot turn a runway 11 into a runway 12. The 1978 sheet still saying 11/29, with 12/30 only in the late CFS-era description, fits a later variation drop rather than a new runway in the 1940s or 1950s. This is not a renumbering date.
- The 1942 “new runway” in the LAC minutes may be one of the three already listed in the c.1942 manual, or a lengthening of one of them. The minutes do not give a number, so it is not identified here.

### Gaps
- No aerodrome directory, CFS, or *Canada Air Pilot* airport chart was found for 1950, 1960, or 1970 that would show whether 15/33 had already become 16/34, or whether 03/21 and 11/29 kept those numbers through the 1950s and 1960s.
- The year 11/29 was redesignated 12/30 was not found. It is after the 1978 *On Track* sheet.
- Displaced-threshold distances and which runway was the calm-wind runway before the 1978 noise note are not in the sources read for this pass.
