# R15 Dawson Bridge — modeling brief

Edmonton. Five-span riveted steel through-truss across the North Saskatchewan River. Opened on 8 October 1912 as the East End Bridge, built for the Dawson coal mine on the east bank. Centre used by the project: 53.549, -113.467. Carries Rowland Road, two lanes.

This is not the High Level Bridge. The High Level is the two-deck bridge on 109 Street, Pratt and Warren spans, and its rail deck is a different height above the river. The Dawson truss is a single deck, and it is a different truss. Do not reuse the High Level model.

Numbers below are cited. Where two sources disagree, both are given. A blank in the "Still missing" list has no published figure in the sources checked. Nothing here was filled in by adding one citation to another. OpenStreetMap `maxheight=4.5` is a mapper tag on the road way. It is not a deck elevation.

## Bridge

**Type.** Riveted steel through-truss, five simply supported spans (Lima, DiBattista, and Kanji, 2011; the 2011 award submission). HistoricBridges.org names the families: three Camelback through-truss spans, one Pennsylvania through-truss span, and one small Pratt through-truss span. The same page's structure line calls the main span a 14-panel rivet-connected Pennsylvania through truss and calls the approach spans Camelback, which does not mention the Pratt. Both wordings are on that page. The engineering papers say through-truss and do not name Camelback, Pennsylvania, or Pratt.

The elevation photographs show the same arrangement: three lower polygonal spans, one higher polygonal span, and one shorter, lower span. The 2016 sidewalk photograph shows subdivided panels and cross-bracing in the higher span. A panel count for the Camelback spans and for the Pratt span is not published. HistoricBridges states 14 panels for the Pennsylvania span only.

**Spans.** From west to east, 43.3 m, 43.3 m, 43.3 m, 76.2 m, and 30.5 m (2011 paper and the award submission). The 30.5 m span is the east approach span. The 76.2 m span is the navigation span. That name is the span's length, not a height above the water. The City's general arrangement, reproduced in both of those PDFs, is drawn at 1 inch = 40 feet and labels the same five spans 142 ft, 142 ft, 142 ft, 250 ft, and 100 ft. HistoricBridges states the main span as 250.0 feet (76.2 m).

**Length.** 236.5 m between abutment walls (2011 paper; the award submission's history section). The award submission's project narrative also says a total length of 236.6 m. HistoricBridges states 776.0 feet (236.5 m). Wikipedia and Structurae state 236.5 m. The 236.5 m and the 236.6 m are both published. They were not averaged.

The OpenStreetMap road centreline, way 22916476, is a straight line 237.1 m long. The mapped footprint, way 642360972, has long sides of 236.2 m. Use 236.5 m as the length between abutment walls. Use the OpenStreetMap lines for the plan shape. The mapped length and the length between abutment walls are different extents.

**Truss.** Built-up members: steel angles, cover plates, gusset plates, and lattice webs. Original connections are 19 mm or 22 mm rivets. The train was on the south lane and the vehicles were on the north lane, so the south truss members are about 10 percent heavier than the north truss (2011 paper). Truss depth is not published.

HistoricBridges names the builder as the Dominion Bridge Company of Montréal, and says at least some of the steel was British, from Dorman Long. The engineering papers do not name the builder or the mill. Construction cost was $145,000 (award submission and HistoricBridges).

**Piers.** Four concrete piers on concrete spread footings in the river, and an abutment at each bank (2011 paper). The photographs show solid concrete shafts standing in the channel, wider toward the water. Pier height is not published. A footing size is not published.

**Clearance above the river.** Not published. No navigation clearance is published. No elevation above sea level is published for the deck, the soffit, or the water. The general arrangement reproduced in the 2011 paper and the award PDF has small figures along the piers and the water line. They are not legible in that reproduction, and they were not transcribed. Environment Canada gauge 05DF001 is at the Low Level Bridge and uses a local assumed datum. No source used here converts a Dawson clearance from that gauge.

**Rehabilitation that shows.** The truss outline was kept. In 2010 about 40 percent of the main truss members were strengthened or replaced. New members were built to the same dimensions as the old ones. The lattice look was kept by plasma-cut holes in a solid web plate (award submission). The sidewalks were not kept at the old width. They went from 1.5 m to 2.65 m, on new brackets detailed to the old geometry (2011 paper and the award submission). That is a wider deck edge, not a new truss.

The bridge closed on 4 January 2010 and reopened on 20 December 2010. Earlier decks, which are gone: timber in 1912, timber plank with asphalt in 1926, concrete on creosote planks in 1942, and in 1986 a 165 mm semi-lightweight concrete deck on the existing 50 mm wood sub-deck, thinned to 65 mm over the floor beams. The 1986 work also replaced the expansion bearings with pot bearings. The mine closed in 1944, and after that the bridge carried highway traffic only.

The City's 2025–2026 work strengthened the piers, repaired cracks, repaired the abutments, and replaced the barriers. It was finished in late September 2026. Two-way traffic had returned in late July 2026. The City does not describe a new truss outline or a new pier shape. It does not describe the new barrier. Swing stages were still on the underside after the September 2026 note, to be removed in the winter. The 2016 sidewalk photograph shows a lattice railing. That photograph is older than the barrier replacement.

The City's 2026 project page and the May 2026 council report list the bridge on the Inventory of Historic Resources. HistoricBridges says it was designated a Municipal Historic Resource. No Dawson Bridge record was found on HistoricPlaces.ca. The High Level Bridge and the Mill Creek Trestle are the Edmonton bridges that search returned there.

## Approaches

The sources divide the ends by bank, west and east, rather than by north and south. Riverdale is the west end. Forest Heights is the east end. The coal mine was on the east bank. The OpenStreetMap road centreline runs from the Riverdale end to the Forest Heights end, east-northeast.

No source gives the present gradient, the elevation change, or the point where either road meets natural grade. The OpenStreetMap ways below are complete ways: each one has the same node count as the live way on 14 October 2026. A complete way is still only the mapped polyline. Its far node is not a surveyed grade point.

**West, Riverdale.** Way 934518260 is Rowland Road NW from 87 Street to the west end of the deck: two lanes, asphalt, 50 km/h, width tag 10, 21.4 m. Way 934518262 is the next Rowland Road way to the southwest: two lanes, asphalt, 50 km/h, width tag 10, 135.7 m. The extract calls 934518262 a cross road because it does not share a node with the bridge way. It is the same road. 87 Street NW and 88 Street NW meet this road at the west end. They are cross streets. They are not the climb.

**East, Forest Heights.** The road leaves the east end as two one-way carriageways, each tagged one lane, asphalt, 50 km/h, width 4.

- Way 934518263, 48.5 m, node order away from the bridge, so the one-way is eastbound off the deck.
- Way 303529114, 112.3 m, node order toward the bridge, so the one-way is westbound onto the deck.

The two ways are not the same length. Neither far node is the top of the bank. A July 2006 photograph looks down a curving two-lane road with a concrete barrier and chevron signs, toward the downtown towers. The bridge is not in the frame. That is this approach as it was in 2006. No gradient was measured from it.

## Deck

**Elevation.** Missing above sea level, and the vertical datum is missing. The 2010 deck was detailed to clear the tops of the existing floor beams without raising the grade line (2011 paper and the award submission). No figure for that grade line is given.

**Thickness.** The 2010 roadway is a sandwich plate: 10 mm steel, 25 mm elastomer, 10 mm steel, 45 mm in all. The award submission calls the face plates 350AT. The panels are typically 1.90 m wide by 8.6 m long (2011 paper) and typically 1.9 m wide by 8.5 m long (award submission). Both sizes are published. Five new lines of W460×74 stringers carry the panels. That designation is the stringer, not the depth from the wearing surface to the soffit. Floor-beam depth is not published. The depth from the asphalt to the soffit is not published.

The steel deck in each span is planar, with no crossfall. The asphalt wears from 40 mm at the edges to 100 mm at the centreline (2011 paper). The award submission says 100 mm at the crown and 40 mm at the shoulders. A crossfall slope is not published. Longitudinal grade on the span is not published.

**Width.** Clear width 8.1 m (2011 paper and the award submission). That is the published clear width. It is not a lane width. Lane width is missing. Shoulder width is missing.

Wikipedia's infobox says the bridge is 12.5 m wide and does not cite a measurement for that width. Structurae also states 12.5 m. The 8.1 m and the 12.5 m are different published numbers. They were not reconciled here.

OpenStreetMap way 22916476 tags `width=7` and `maxheight=4.5`. No published source found states 7 m or 4.5 m. The 2011 paper and the award submission both mention vertical clearance restrictions for vehicles on the bridge, and neither gives the clearance. The 4.5 tag is not a deck elevation. The mapped footprint is 14.4 m across at each end. That is the OpenStreetMap outline, not a published structural width.

**Lanes.** Two, one in each direction, asphalt (City; the 2011 papers; OpenStreetMap way 22916476). The way is tagged 50 km/h. Node order on the bridge way is from the Riverdale end to the Forest Heights end. `sidewalk=separate`.

**Sidewalks.** One on each side, outside the trusses, on brackets. After 2010 each is wood, 2.65 m clear width. Before 2010 each was 1.5 m (2011 paper and the award submission).

OpenStreetMap splits them:

- Way 303529127, wood, tagged cycleway, foot and bicycle designated, 236.1 m. Followed from Riverdale to Forest Heights, it lies on the north side of the road centreline.
- Way 451267263, asphalt, tagged footway, 236.0 m. It lies on the south side of that centreline.

The 2016 photograph shows a wood-plank sidewalk with a lattice railing. The design sidewalks are wood on both sides. The asphalt tag on the south way is not confirmed by the papers. The June 2026 barrier replacement is later than that photograph.

## Still missing

- Deck elevation above sea level, and the vertical datum. Soffit elevation.
- Clearance from the roadway or the soffit down to the river, and a navigation clearance. The 76.2 m figure is the length of the navigation span. The small figures on the 1912 elevation were not legible in the published reproduction.
- Truss depth. Panel counts for the three Camelback spans and for the east Pratt span. HistoricBridges states 14 panels for the Pennsylvania span only.
- Pier heights, and the plan size of the spread footings.
- Floor-beam depth, and the depth from the wearing surface to the soffit. The 45 mm panel, the 40 mm to 100 mm asphalt, and the W460×74 stringer designation are published. They are not that depth.
- Lane width and shoulder width. What the Wikipedia and Structurae 12.5 m width measures.
- A published confirmation of the OpenStreetMap tags `width=7` and `maxheight=4.5`. The 4.5 is not a deck elevation.
- Crossfall as a slope. Longitudinal grade on the spans.
- Where the Riverdale road and the Forest Heights road meet natural grade, and the length, gradient, and elevation change of each climb.
- Whether the June 2026 barrier replacement changed the lattice railing in the 2016 photograph. The City does not describe the new barrier.
- An openly licensed underside photograph, and an openly licensed aerial.
