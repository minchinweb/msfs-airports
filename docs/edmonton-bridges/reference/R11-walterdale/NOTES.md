# R11 Walterdale Bridge — modeling brief

Edmonton. Twin through-arch across the North Saskatchewan River. Traffic opened on 18 September 2017 with two lanes. All three lanes and the west sidewalk opened on 29 September 2017, and the east shared-use path opened later that fall. The City's grand opening was September 2018. Centre used by the project: 53.529, -113.502. Carries 105 Street northbound.

The 1913 truss stood just west of this alignment and was demolished in 2017. It is not part of this bridge.

The project list calls this a tied arch. The design paper (Montgomery, Gagnon, Clare, Lima, Ghoneim, Griezic, and Kanji, 2014) says the team chose a thrust arch, and that a thrust arch was the better structure. The 2019 award submission, written after the bridge was up, describes a single-span twin through-arch whose ribs land on thrust blocks. Tension ties were used to hold the arch during the lifts, and they were removed. The finished deck has no tie.

Numbers below are cited. Where two sources disagree, both are given. A blank in the "Still missing" list has no published figure in the sources checked. Nothing here was filled in by adding one citation to another.

## Bridge

**Span.** 206 m from bank to bank, and 230 m for the deck between abutment centrelines. Those two lengths are in the 2014 design paper, the 2019 award submission, Pedelta, and the designer's project page (the page states the 206 m; the 230 m is the paper and the award submission). The City's 2013 yearbook and the archived 2014 features page also state the 206 m arch length.

An October 2012 site location study, written before detailed design, said the crossing would span approximately 240 m at a 15° angle, skewing eastward. That 240 m and that 15° were not repeated as as-built figures in the design papers. The OpenStreetMap road centreline, way 175326660, is 260.7 m horizontally, which is longer than 230 m. The mapped way and the abutment-to-abutment length are different extents. Use 230 m as the structural length between abutment centrelines. Use the OpenStreetMap polyline for the plan shape. The extra mapped length is unexplained; it may include pavement past the abutment centres.

**Arch rise.** The 56 m figure is the City's height for the arches, and the 2013 yearbook says the arches rise 56 m above the thrust blocks. The same figure is on the City's current project page ("two 56-metre-tall arches"), on the archived 2014 features page, on the DIALOG project page ("56 m tall"), and in the Wikipedia article, which cites that features page. It is not a rise measured from the roadway.

The rise above the deck is a different number, and the sources do not agree:

- 2013 yearbook diagram: arch height 43 m, on the same graphic as the 13.3 m mid-span height above water.
- Kris Lima, interviewed in 2013: the arch extends 43 m above the bridge deck, or about 55 m above normal river level.
- ACCIONA's Australia project page: 43 m above the deck and 55 m above the river.
- 2019 award submission, from the design team after construction: the arch ribs rise 40 m above the roadway.
- City project update, October 2015, during construction: the arches will be 54 m tall. Pedelta also says the arches are 54 m tall.
- City project page, April 2016 lift: the arch was raised to an ultimate height of 54 m above the river.
- ACCIONA's project article says the arch was lifted to 54 m above the river and, in another sentence, that the arch is 54 m measured from the deck.

43 m above the deck and 40 m above the roadway are both published. 56 m above the thrust blocks, about 55 m above normal river level, and 54 m above the river are also all published. They were not averaged here. For the rib itself, the yearbook's 56 m above the thrust blocks is the figure that matches the City's "56 m tall", and the 43 m is the rise above the deck on the yearbook diagram and in Lima's interview. The award submission's 40 m above the roadway remains on the record beside them.

**Arch cross-section.** Box sections of structural steel plate. The 2014 paper and the 2019 award submission: about 2.5 m by 2.5 m with 100 mm plate at the base, to 1.4 m by 1.4 m with 75 mm plate at the crown. Lima's 2013 interview gives the same taper in round numbers and calls it a diameter; the design papers call it a box. Each rib is 21 segments, numbered from 1 at the south concrete support leg (2019 award submission). DIALOG says 42 segments for the two ribs, the heaviest 125 tonnes, with 1,500 bolts at each connection. The ribs are curved between splices, not faceted (2014 paper).

**Inclination.** 13.5° from vertical when the bridge is viewed in section (2014 paper). Lima's interview says about 14° off vertical. The ribs lean toward each other. The 15° in the 2012 study is a plan skew of the proposed alignment, not this lean.

**Hangers.** The road deck hangs in two planes. Pedelta: 16 hangers in each plane, 32 in all, spaced at 10 m centres. DIALOG and the City's September 2016 note also say 32 hangers for the deck. The shared-use path has its own plane from the east arch. Pedelta says 16 hangers in that plane. The City's July 2016 construction note says there will be 14 hangers for the shared-use path. Both counts are kept.

The 2014 paper describes the hangers as parallel-strand stay cables inside a high-density polyethylene pipe, fixed at the upper fork and adjustable at the lower fork. Near mid-span the shared-use path is also carried by floor-beam extensions from the road deck. Near the banks it sits on delta piers and on its own abutments. The road-deck edge girders rest on bearings at the abutments and on beams between the arch ribs where the girders pass the ribs (2014 paper). Struts connect the two ribs above the roadway (2014 paper and the 2019 award submission). A strut count is not given.

**Piers in the river.** None. The City project page, the 2013 yearbook, the 2014 features page, the 2019 award submission, and Lima's interview all say the thrust-arch span removes piers from the river. The shared-use path does have delta piers "near the water's edge" (2019 award submission) and "adjacent to the water" (2014 paper). The underside photograph shows those piers on the riprap bank, with open water in front of them. Their number is not published. They are bank supports for the path, not piers in the channel.

**Thrust blocks and abutments.** Four thrust blocks, two on each bank, one under each arch foot. They are buried. What shows above the bank is a cast-in-place concrete support leg, stressed to the rib (2019 award submission, and the support-leg photograph).

The buried block is described differently:

- City features page and DIALOG: about 600 m³ of concrete, 20 m below the road elevation, 44 micropiles.
- City updates in August 2014 and October 2015: 10 m across, 20 m below the road. The August update also says the concrete slope is set at 45 degrees. The 2013 yearbook prints the block size as "10 m 2" and the depth as 20 m below the road elevation.
- 2019 award submission: inclined footing 9 m by 10.5 m, 44 micropiles of 200 mm diameter embedded 18 m into the clay shale, braced excavation 18 m deep and about 16 m by 14 m. The support leg is attached with high-strength threaded bars described as "58-65 mm diameter". That phrase is ambiguous as printed.
- 2014 paper: excavations about 18 m deep, plan about 15.5 m by 14.2 m, in clay shale.
- Lima, 2013: 400 m³, 15 m below grade. That volume and that depth disagree with the later City and award figures.

Abutments are separate from the thrust blocks. The features page: cast-in-place and precast concrete on drilled piles, retaining the bank and supporting the bridge. The yearbook: drilled concrete piles and retaining walls. Lima said the arch foundations sit 12 m closer to the water than the abutments, which leaves a path under the bridge. Gabion walls take up the grade change from the bridge down to the bank (2019 award submission). Flood lines are stamped on the abutment walls. The photograph of them shows the years 1915 and 1986. DIALOG says there are fewer markings on the north abutment because the north bank is higher. No elevation is printed on the markers in the sources used here.

**Clearance above the river.** The only figure is the 2013 yearbook diagram: "Height above water (mid-span): 13.3 m". The diagram does not say whether that is to the roadway or to the soffit, and it does not name the water level. It is a design-stage label, not an as-built survey. No navigation clearance is published. No elevation above sea level is published for the deck, the soffit, or the water.

## Approaches

The road approaches were rebuilt with the bridge. Queen Elizabeth Park Road and Walterdale Hill Road were realigned on the south side. On the north side the 2012 study called the work minor modifications to 105 Street. No source gives the present gradient, the elevation change, or the point where either road meets natural grade.

Paths beyond the plazas are accessible at slopes less than 5% (2019 award submission). That sentence is about the trails, not the roadways.

**North.** The 2012 study starts the alignment at the intersection of 105 Street and River Valley Road, then takes it south on a slight realignment of 105 Street onto the bridge. No length of climb and no rise are given for that hill. DIALOG says the north bank is higher than the south bank and does not say by how much.

OpenStreetMap way 471835847 is the road leaving the north end of the deck: 105 Street NW, five lanes, one-way, asphalt, 50 km/h, 112.3 m horizontally, from the bridge at 53.529505, -113.501139 to 53.530513, -113.501096. The extract cuts the way at the edge of the downloaded box. The far node is not a surveyed grade point, and it is not confirmed as River Valley Road. The deck itself is tagged as three lanes, so the road widens on this north way. Elevation along it is not in the extract.

A separate cycleway, way 302444162, continues the west sidewalk north for 96.6 m and is also clipped. It is not the road climb.

**South.** The 2012 study takes the alignment across part of Dantzer's Hill in Queen Elizabeth Park and forks it into Walterdale Hill Road and Queen Elizabeth Park Road. Each of those roads was to be realigned approximately 300 m. That is a length of roadwork in the study, not a measured vertical climb, and it predates construction. The 2019 award submission says Walterdale Hill Road was narrowed from three lanes to two from 109 Street northward, so that a shared-use path could be built along its north side.

OpenStreetMap still tags the two ways that touch the south end of the deck as three lanes. They are Walterdale Hill NW, one-way, asphalt, 50 km/h:

- Way 931254808, 31.3 m, from 53.526834, -113.502512 to 53.527101, -113.502365.
- Way 1516540140, 19.0 m, from there to the south end of the road deck at 53.527264, -113.502281.

The extract called them cross roads because the street name is not the bridge name. They are the south road, and they are clipped at the download box. Their far end is not the top of the hill. Queen Elizabeth Park Road is the other south fork in the 2012 study. It is not a second deck, and it is not in the bridge ways kept here.

No gradient and no elevation change are published for either south road.

## Deck

**Elevation.** Missing above sea level, and the vertical datum is missing. The only height tied to the water is the yearbook's 13.3 m at mid-span, with the limits given under Bridge. The mean-water elevation itself is not in these sources, so the 13.3 m cannot be turned into an elevation above sea level from the material here.

**Thickness.** Missing. The road deck is a concrete slab on steel wide-flange stringers, plate-girder floor beams of varying depth, and box edge girders, with an asphalt wearing surface on a waterproof membrane (2014 paper and 2019 award submission). No slab thickness and no asphalt thickness are given. The shared-use path is a trapezoidal steel box girder 1.4 m deep (2019 award submission), with a polymer-modified asphalt surface and wood accents on the east side (2014 paper). That 1.4 m is the path girder, not the road-deck thickness. Pedelta calls the path girder a tube box of variable width.

**Width.** Pedelta: the traffic deck is 22.4 m wide and holds three lanes, two shoulders, barriers, and a sidewalk. ACCIONA's article says the deck is 22.5 m wide. ACCIONA's Australia page says 22.40 m and then places the walkway and the shared-use path on the opposite sides from the designer. The 22.4 m is the traffic deck, sidewalk included, not the traffic deck plus the separated path and the gap.

Lane width is missing. Shoulder width is missing. The west sidewalk width is missing. The sidewalk is on the west, inside the plane of the hangers. The structure can take a future fourth lane on that side, with a new sidewalk built outside the cables (2014 paper and 2019 award submission). That lane is not there now.

**Lanes.** Three, northbound, asphalt, 50 km/h (City; OpenStreetMap ways 175326660 and 471835847). Node order on the bridge way is south to north.

**Shared-use path.** East of the road, separated from it. Clear width 8 m at the banks and 4.2 m near mid-span (2014 paper and 2019 award submission). The City's July 2016 note says the path is up to 9 m wide at the ends and 4.2 m in the centre. "Clear width" and "up to nine metres" may be different measurements; both are recorded. The gap between the path and the road deck runs from 8 m to 2 m (Lima, 2013). The path is wider at the banks and closer to the road near mid-span. OpenStreetMap way 451293733 is that path: 275.4 m horizontally, 22 nodes, tagged `surface=concrete`. The design wearing surface is asphalt. The west sidewalk is way 709287392, 266.8 m, also tagged concrete, which matches a concrete sidewalk more closely than the east path does.

A bench runs along the middle 50 percent of the shared-use path, on its west side, with a solid back toward the traffic (2014 paper). DIALOG says the path is about twice as wide at each end as in the middle, which agrees in direction with 8 m at the banks and 4.2 m at mid-span.

**Crossfall.** Missing. No longitudinal grade on the span is published either. The 2014 paper says the deck expands and contracts about the centre of the structure.

## Still missing

- Deck elevation above sea level, and the vertical datum. Also the elevation of the water surface used for the 13.3 m label.
- Whether 13.3 m is to the roadway or to the soffit, and a navigation clearance.
- A single arch rise. 56 m above the thrust blocks, 43 m and 40 m above the deck or roadway, and 55 m and 54 m above the river are all published.
- Present road-deck thickness, asphalt thickness, crossfall, and longitudinal grade.
- Lane width, shoulder width, and west sidewalk width.
- Shared-use-path hanger count. Pedelta says 16 and the City's 2016 note says 14.
- Delta-pier count, and a strut count.
- Where the north and south roads meet grade, and the length, gradient, and elevation change of each climb. The OpenStreetMap approach ways are clipped and have no elevations.
- As-built plan skew. The 15° figure is from the 2012 study.
- An openly licensed aerial or orthophoto.
