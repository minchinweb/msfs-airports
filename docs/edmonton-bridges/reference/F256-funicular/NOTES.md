# F256 100 Street Funicular — modeling brief

Edmonton. The 100 Street funicular. Global News calls it Edmonton's river valley funicular. DIALOG calls it Edmonton's funicular. During construction the project name was Mechanized River Valley Access. The lookout is the Frederick G. Todd Lookout. Wikipedia calls that deck the Frederick G. Todd Overlook. The City name is Lookout.

It is on the north bank. The pedestrian bridge crosses above Grierson Hill Road. The mapped footbridge is north-northeast of the Low Level north end. The upper end of the mapped track is northwest of that end, at 100 Street, south of B255, the Hotel Macdonald. The hotel brief is in `../B255-hotel-macdonald/`. This is not the Grierson Hill Bridge. That span is way 106711211, centre 53.538564, -113.488789, and it carries Grierson Hill Road over McDougall Hill Road. This is not a Low Level deck. The 1900 northbound deck is way 31882107. The 1948/1949 southbound deck is way 22916473. The code is F256. The track, the Frederick G. Todd Lookout, and the footbridge over Grierson Hill Road are this one code.

Numbers below are cited. Where two sources disagree, both are given. A blank in the "Still missing" list has no published figure in the sources checked. Nothing here was filled in by adding one citation to another, or by turning a slope length into a plan length, or a plan length into a slope length. OpenStreetMap `width` tags are mapper tags. They are not a deck width. No elevation was stored.

## The inclined railway

**What it is.** One cabin. An inclined elevator, cable-propelled, with no operator on board (City of Edmonton project page; DIALOG). The Edmonton Journal, 8 December 2017, calls it a Plexiglas-enclosed car. The City calls it glass-enclosed. The Journal, 7 March 2016, calls it a glass cabin. A rider quoted by Global News on 9 December 2017 calls it a glass box. No source read here describes a second car, or a pair of counterbalanced cars.

**Where the ends sit.** The upper station is the promontory at the top of the bank. The award submission says the promontory is an extension of 100 Street. The City address for the facility is 10007 - 100 Street NW. The Journal, 7 March 2016 and 8 December 2017, places the work south of the Hotel Macdonald at 10065 100 St. The lower station is the promenade, mid-way along the valley slope, not the valley floor (DIALOG award submission; CISC). The funicular runs from the promontory to that promenade. An elevator and stairs, at the lookout, continue to the trails.

The mapped track is OpenStreetMap way 490235928, tagged `railway=funicular` and `name=100 Street Funicular`. It is a straight line of two nodes. The north node, 53.5396888, -113.4896381, is the upper end: it is the end toward 100 Street and away from the river, and the stair ways beside it are tagged `incline=down` in that direction. The south node, 53.5394103, -113.4887917, is the lower end, at the promenade. No elevation is on the way.

**Track length.** Two published lengths, and one mapped length. They were not averaged, and none was converted into another.

The Edmonton Journal, 7 March 2016, says a 55-metre incline railway. The same paper, 8 December 2017, says a 55-metre incline cable railway. Paula Simons, 8 April 2015, quotes Rob Marchak on a 55-metre track. That 2015 sentence is before construction.

The DIALOG award submission, and the CISC account of the same project, state the funicular track as 66.0 m long along a 23.5 degree grade from horizontal. That 66.0 m is a length along the grade. It is not a plan length. A vertical rise was not computed from it.

Way 490235928 is 64.0 m. That figure is the horizontal length of the two mapped nodes, WGS84, on 14 October 2026. It is not the 55 m incline, and it is not the 66.0 m along the grade. The way has no `incline` tag and no `gauge` tag. The line is straight because it has two nodes. A curve was not read from it.

**Vertical rise.** Not published for the built track. On 8 April 2015, before construction, Paula Simons quoted Marchak: a 25-metre drop, on the 55-metre track. The March 2016 Journal repeats the 55 metres and does not repeat the 25 metres. The December 2017 Journal does not state a drop. The award submission does not state a rise for the track. The 25 m was not used as the as-built rise.

The award submission also says the top of the river bank is cut off from the trails by an elevation change of 50 m. CISC uses the same sentence. That 50 m is the valley. It is not the funicular. It was not subtracted from anything.

**Gradient.** 23.5 degrees from horizontal (award submission; CISC). A percent grade is not published. The 23.5 degrees were not turned into a rise.

**Cars.** One. Capacity 20 people (City; Journal, 7 March 2016 and 8 December 2017; DIALOG). The City states the cabin as 3 metres by 2.2 metres. The page does not say which figure is the length. It carries wheelchairs, strollers, and bikes. DIALOG says it is long enough for an adult bike with a child trailer, and that riders keep the same direction: they enter and leave without turning around.

**Speed.** Top speed 2 metres per second (award submission; CISC). DIALOG says the maximum speed is about 2 m/s. The Journal, 8 December 2017, says the car takes 48 seconds from the top to the walkway. The 48 seconds and the 2 m/s were not divided into a length.

**Gauge.** Not published. The 24 September 2026 photographs show two rails on a steel beam. No gauge was measured from them.

**Guide structure.** The award submission says the funicular traverses the slope on elevated steel rails and beams. CISC says it is supported by steel rails. The same award section says the structure on the slope is supported on steel screw piles. That sentence sits with the stairs. It was not separated into a pile under the rails and a pile under the treads. The upper landing is a canopy of galvanized steel with glass and wood, over below-grade concrete machine and electrical rooms (award submission). Concrete piles under that upper structure resist horizontal forces from the funicular. Norfab fabricated the funicular canopies and the railings (CISC). There is no tower along the track in these accounts. The glass shaft at the lookout is the elevator. It is not the funicular guide.

The 24 September 2026 photographs show the one cabin, the two rails, the steel beam under the rails, and the stair beside them. The upper canopy is at the top of the rail line in the wider frame. No height was read from either frame.

## The pedestrian bridge

This bridge is part of the funicular project. It stays in this folder. It is the span the Grierson Hill Bridge notes set aside.

**What it crosses.** Grierson Hill Road. The City says the bridge gives passage across Grierson Hill Road and connects the promenade to the lookout. Global News, 9 December 2017, gives the order as a staircase, then a promenade, then a pedestrian bridge over Grierson Hill, with a lookout. Way 1271848915 crosses way 106711201. It does not cross way 106711211. That crossing was checked on the live nodes on 14 October 2026.

**Year the steel went in.** SkyriseCities, 5 March 2020, says the steel bridge over Grierson Hill Road, part of the 100 Street funicular and the Frederick G. Todd Lookout, was installed overnight on 17 October 2016. No source read here gives a different night for that steel. Construction of the project began in March 2016 (SkyriseCities). The Journal, 7 March 2016, says construction begins that week. The award submission says the project was completed in December 2017. The 17 October 2016 date is the steel. It is not the day the public crossed the deck. The award submission says the handrails went on in the winter, and a cast-in-place topping went on in the summer, with the remaining Kebony platforms.

**Structure.** SkyriseCities calls it a steel bridge. The award submission says two girders of notch-tough weathering steel, fabricated by Supreme Steel, with steel bracing. CISC says the same two girders. The concrete bridge deck is precast segments about 3 m long, grouted to the girders, with a cementitious topping and Kebony platforms (award submission). The 3 m is the segment length along the bridge. It is not a width. Galvanized steel railings, with Kebony cladding, are on the bridge (award submission).

Way 1271848915 is tagged `bridge:structure=beam` and `man_made=bridge`. It has no name. The `beam` tag is the mapper's tag. The award submission does not use the word beam. It says two girders.

The cycleway on the outline, way 533867748, is tagged `surface=concrete`. That tag is not a published deck specification. The 15 December 2017 photograph shows a wood-coloured deck. The award submission says precast concrete, a cementitious topping, and Kebony platforms. Kebony is a treated softwood (award submission, in the stair section). The 24 March 2018 photograph, at the elevator, shows a grey deck. The wood-coloured span and the grey landing were not averaged into one surface.

**Spans.** 50.0 m main span, 19.4 m span, and 19.0 m cantilever (award submission; CISC writes them as 50.0 m main span – 19.4 m span – 19.0 m cantilever). Those three figures were not added. A total structure length is not published.

**Mapped length.** Way 533867748 is the cycleway on the outline. On the live nodes of 14 October 2026 its horizontal length is 91.0 m. The Grierson Hill Bridge notes record 91.1 m for this way. The 91.0 m and the 91.1 m were not averaged. Neither is the 50.0 m main span. The outline, way 1271848915, has a perimeter of 191.0 m. That perimeter is the ring. It is not a span length. The centreline bends. The 15 December 2017 photograph shows a kink in the deck. The mapped centreline also bends, in the short segments between the two longer runs. No angle was published, and none was taken off the photograph.

**Piers.** The award submission says the steel girders are supported on a concrete pier and a steel-framed elevator shaft. CISC says a concrete pier and a steel elevator shaft. A second concrete pier is not described. A column size is not published. A pier height is not published. The 15 December 2017 photograph shows a concrete pier under the deck. No count was made from the frame beyond that one pier in view.

**Clearance.** The City says the bridge is approximately 6 m above the roadway. The award submission says the clearance was raised to be higher than nearby bridges, and that it exceeds typical clearance requirements. It does not give the clearance. The approximate 6 m and the sentence without a number were not turned into one figure. Way 1271848915 has no `maxheight` tag. Way 106711201, the road underneath, has no `maxheight` tag. The 6 m is not a deck elevation.

**Width.** Not published for the bridge deck. The OpenStreetMap bridge ways have no `width` tag.

## Lookout, stair, and promenade

Only the parts that change the silhouette are here.

**Lookout.** The south end of the bridge cantilevers past the elevator shaft (award submission). The award submission states the length of the lookout cantilever as 19 m, and the height of the lookout above the river bank as 19 m. It also calls the lookout a 19 m high lookout. CISC states each of the two fact-box lines as 19 m (60 feet), and calls it a 19 m high lookout. The 19 m and the 60 feet are CISC's pair. They were not converted here. The 19 m above the river bank is not a height above the river, and it is not a height above sea level.

The City says the lookout has a glass railing and built-in seating. The 24 March 2018 photograph shows the glass elevator shaft on the deck, with metal railings along that landing, and the funicular stair on the hill behind it. The 8 August 2017 photograph, during construction, shows a steel cantilever out of the trees on the bank, with the Low Level truss at the left of the frame and the Hotel Macdonald at the top of the bank. The 26 May 2018 photograph shows a railed deck above the trees, over a trail. No height was read from any of them. The elevator holds up to 10 people (City; Journal, 7 March 2016). The shaft is glass, and it supports the bridge without bearings (award submission).

**Urban stair.** Parallel to the funicular. The City says it is between 4.5 metres and 7.5 metres wide, with a bike rail. The Journal, 7 March 2016, states the same width, and says the treads are Kebony, with concrete seating. The award submission says the treads are Kebony, the benches are hollow precast concrete, the stringers are galvanized steel, and the stairs hover above the slope. Norfab fabricated the stringers (CISC). The 19 January 2018 photograph shows wood treads, concrete benches, a glass rail on one side, and a metal rail with vertical lights on the other, with the Low Level truss beyond. The 24 September 2026 photographs show that stair beside the rails. No width was measured from them.

Ways 533867743 and 1301170658 are the mapped steps beside the track. Both are tagged `surface=wood`, `incline=down`, and `width=6`. The horizontal lengths are 37.9 m and 22.7 m. They do not share a node. The gap between the end of way 533867743 and the start of way 1301170658 is 3.9 m, map distance. The south end of way 1301170658 is 5.6 m from the lower track node, map distance. Those lengths were not added. The `width=6` tag is not the City's 4.5 to 7.5 metres. No published source found states 6 m as the stair width.

The award submission says the walk from the river-valley trail to the promontory is 250 stairs. That count is the whole walk. It is not a count for the urban stair alone. It is not a height.

**Promenade.** The Journal, 8 December 2017, says the car runs to a 200-metre walkway partway down the hill, which leads to the pedestrian bridge. The City says the total walk from the top of the urban stair to the bottom landing by the trails is approximately 260 metres (850 feet). The 200 metres and the 260 metres are different published walks. They were not averaged. On 8 April 2015 Marchak described a gently sloping six-metre-wide boardwalk. That 6 m is a 2015 width for the boardwalk. It is not the City's approximate 6 m clearance over the road, and it was not used as the bridge width. The award submission says the promenade is a Kebony boardwalk on steel screw piles, beside a lawn, with the steel artwork Turbulent.

No way in the set read on 14 October 2026 joins the lower track node to the north node of way 533867748. The horizontal distance between those two nodes is 55.3 m. That gap is a map distance. It is not the 200-metre walkway.

Two short step ways sit at the bridge. Way 535658874 is 13.6 m, shares a node with the outline, and is tagged `incline=down`. Way 622160716 is 14.4 m. It does not share a node. Its nearer end is 2.8 m from an outline node. Neither has a surface tag or a step count. They were not added to the urban stair.

## Plan position

These are horizontal map distances, WGS84, radius 6378137 m, on the live coordinates of 14 October 2026. They are not clearances and not slope lengths.

The centre used here is the mean of the 12 corners of way 1271848915, the closing node not counted twice: 53.53930454, -113.48747175. Given to six decimal places as 53.539305, -113.487472.

The Grierson Hill Bridge midpoint, 53.538564, -113.488789, is the midpoint of way 106711211. The footbridge centroid is 120.0 m from that midpoint, on a bearing of 46.6 degrees, northeast. The Grierson notes record the same 120.0 m.

The north node of the 1900 Low Level deck, way 31882107, is 53.5384329, -113.4880941. The footbridge centroid is 105.4 m from that node, bearing 23.0 degrees, north-northeast. The north node of the southbound deck, way 22916473, is 53.5383520, -113.4881657. The centroid is 115.5 m from that node, bearing 23.4 degrees. The midpoint of those two north nodes is 110.5 m from the centroid, bearing 23.2 degrees. The southeast node of the outline, 53.5390925, -113.4870148, is the river end of the mapped deck. It is 102.4 m from the 1900 north node, bearing 44.2 degrees, northeast.

The upper track node is 173.1 m from the 1900 north node, bearing 323.8 degrees, northwest. It is 137.2 m from the Grierson midpoint, bearing 335.8 degrees. The lower track node is 94.2 m from the Grierson midpoint, bearing 359.9 degrees, north. It is 118.2 m from the 1900 north node, bearing 337.0 degrees. The midpoint of the two track nodes is 147.5 m from the midpoint of the two Low Level north nodes.

The track and the footbridge are not one straight line. The upper station is up the bank, northwest of the Low Level portal. The footbridge and the lookout are north-northeast and northeast of that portal, over Grierson Hill Road.

## What reads from the air

Include the funicular track, the lookout, and the footbridge in the Low Level north-bank scene.

The 15 December 2017 photograph puts the wood-deck footbridge, its kink, its concrete pier, and the Low Level truss in one frame. The truss is to the right. The road under the footbridge is the hill road. The 19 January 2018 photograph looks down the urban stair and the Low Level truss is the bridge in that view. The 8 August 2017 photograph shows the lookout cantilever on the bank and the truss at the edge of the same frame. The 24 September 2026 photographs show the cabin and the rails on that same slope, beside the stair that looks down on the truss.

The mapped track is 64.0 m on the level. The published track is 55 m, or 66.0 m along the grade. The mapped footbridge centreline is 91.0 m. The lookout cantilever is 19 m. At 1,000 ft those are objects on the bank beside the north portal: a rail line, a bent pedestrian span over the hill road, and a deck stuck out over the bank. At 3,500 ft they are small next to the river truss, and they are still that portal's hillside, not a separate crossing. A north-bank model that stops at the Grierson Hill road deck leaves out the span that crosses the hill road above it, and the track that ends on the slope above that span.

Do not model them as a third river truss. Do not model the footbridge as the Grierson Hill Bridge.
